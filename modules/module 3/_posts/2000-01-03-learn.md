### Module 3: Analyzing Data & interpeting Findings
#### Analyzing Quantitative Data
*Suggested time: ## minutes*

Because quantitative research focuses on numerical data, you use statistical means to analyze the data you collected. The two most frequent types of statistical analyses are **descriptive statistics** and **inferential statistics**. Descriptive statistics, as the name suggests, describe or summarize characteristics of a dataset. Inferential statistics focus on predictions, relationships, and drawing conclusions. While you can certainly use both to analyze the data you collect (either together or independently), typically you will only be using descriptive statistics in your everyday practice. The most common types of descriptive statistics include frequency distribution, measures of central tendency, and measures of variability. 

**Frequency distribution** summarizes how many times a value (such as a person, a book, or a lesson) occurs, and they allow you to see how the data are distributed and to observe patterns. They include total numbers and percentages. You typically display the findings using tables, histograms, bar graphs, and pie charts. You will learn more about displaying findings in Module [insert number and title - communicating]. For example, if you asked 100 9th graders about their favorite book genre, and 20 students (or 20%) prefer horror, 65 students (or 65%) prefer realistic fiction, and 15 students (or 15%) prefer historical fiction, those numbers represent the frequencies. 

**Measures of central tendency** are averages: **mean**, **median**, and **mode**. You would most likely use mean more often than median and mode. When we calculate the average of something, typically we talk about the **mean**: adding up every number in the data set and then dividing by the total number of values. Finding the mean is helpful when the data is distributed evenly. For example, 10 students completed a quiz you developed on digital citizenship. To find the mean score for this quiz, you would add up the score for each student and then divide that number by 10. 

     100 + 90 + 95 + 92 + 78 + 100 + 97 + 98 + 100 + 95 = 945

     945 / 10 = 94.5

In this case, 94.5 is the mean score for your students. 

**Median** is the middle value in a set of numbers. When finding the median, you arrange the numbers in numerical order and select the number in the middle. If you are working with an even set of numbers, then you would add the middle two numbers and divide by two. Median is helpful when your data is skewed either high or low (so it is not evenly distributed), usually by an outlier. An outlier is a number that is an abnormal distance from the other numbers in the group. Calculating the median might be helpful when averaging the number of books checked out in a 3rd grade class, when one student rarely checked out books the entire year, while the other students in the class regularly checked out 1-2 books during each library visit. 

| Student Name  | Number of books checked out for the year |
| ------------- | ------------- |
| Student A  | 10  |
| Student B  | 65 |
| Student C  | 64  |
| Student D  | 62 |
| Student E  | 50  |
| Student F  | 72 |
| Student G  | 71  |
| Student H  | 56 |
| Student I  | 68  |
| Student J  | 51 |
  
To calculate median, first organize the numbers from least to greatest:

     10, 50, 51, 56, 62, 64, 65, 68, 71, 72  

Next, find the middle number. Because there were 10 students in this 3rd grade class (an even number), we will need to select the middle two numbers (the 5th and 6th numbers), add those together, and divide by two. In this example, 62 and 64 are the middle two scores. 

     62 + 64 = 126
     
     126 / 2 =  63

In this example, the median number of books checked out in this 3rd grade class is 63. 

**Mode** is the value that occurs most frequently and is typically used for categorical data as opposed to continuous datasets. Examples of when a school librarian would calculate mode in the school library include, but are not limited to:
* the genre of books checked out most often by students,
* the most frequently attended type of library program,
* the most common student response on a survey, or
* the most frequently used database or digital resource.

For example, a librarian surveys 200 5th grade students about their favorite library activity, and the responses are: reading (98 students), makerspace activities (61 students), coding (28 students), and gaming (13 students). The mode would be reading because it appears most often. In this situation, calculating a mean or median would not be appropriate because the data is categorical rather than numerical.

Finally, we have **measures of variability**, which include **range**, **variance**, and **standard deviation**. In the library setting, you will typically only work with range, which is found by subtracting the lowest value from the highest value. Range helps show how much difference exists within the data set and can reveal whether experiences, behaviors, or outcomes are fairly consistent or highly varied. A librarian might want to calculate the range for technology usage within and across grade levels. You would track the number of times each class uses the library databases in a month. Calculating the range will reveal whether database use is fairly even across grade levels or whether some classes are using the resources far more frequently than others. It is rare that you would calculate the variance and standard deviation. If you would like to learn more about these two measures of variability, please visit the “Explore More” subsection in this module.

> [!NOTE]
You may be wondering about using an AI program to analyze your quantitative data. We warn against that. Why? AI programs are not created to analyze number sets. They are large-language models that use existing data to make predictions based on the existing data. The aforementioned programs, however, are designed to analyze data. Additionally, there are ethical and privacy concerns when uploading research data into an AI program. Note that some of the above programs do integrate AI into their software; however, the tools, with AI integration, are specifically designed to help you analyze your quantitative data. 

#### Analyzing Qualitative Data
When working with non-numerical data, such as open-ended survey questions or interviews, you will want to use qualitative data analysis methods. In the world of scholarly research, there are multiple ways to analyze qualitative data. In EBP, we tend to use coding. Coding is the process of assigning a name or label (i.e., a code) to a block of text - in a way, summarizing what was said, such as a teacher’s response to a survey question about why they have not collaborated with the librarian. 

**WATCH** [Grad Coach Qualitative Coding Tutorial: How To Code Qualitative Data For Analysis (4 Steps + Examples)](url) to learn more about codes and coding  [00:00 - 7:46 minutes]

There are two types of coding commonly used in EBP: in vivo coding and descriptive coding.  In vivo coding is when you assign a code, or label, using the exact language from someone’s response. Descriptive coding is when you assign a code, or label, with a word or short phrase describing what was said; it is also a popular choice when coding photographs or videos. Note that there are other types of coding you could use, but more often than not, in vivo coding and descriptive coding are best suited for your tasks.

For example, a group of school librarians were asked “How do you share evidence with your principal?” One librarian responded with:
>“I also share data out through infographics and announcements, Our principal is awesome. She's always talking about the data. I'll send it to her in just informal ways, and she'll share with others. I also post things on my school library’s TikTok and Instagram accounts, just sharing that information, pulling circ stats.”
The words in this response that answer the question “How do you share evidence with your principal?” include *infographics*, *announcements*, *informal ways*, *TikTok*, and *Instagram*.  

Examples of in vivo codes would be “infographics,” “announcements,” and “informal ways,” while an example of a descriptive code would be *social media*, because social media is a short phrase summarizing the librarian’s use of the social media platforms TikTok and Instagram. Note that you can code the same data set using both in vivo codes and descriptive codes - you are not limited to only using one type within the same data set. Note also that sometimes a response may have only one code, while other times it may have multiple codes.  

Now let’s look at the process for coding. 
1. Organize your data. A list of available tools for analyzing qualitative data are available later in this section. In this situation, you may want to organize your data by question. If you are using a spreadsheet, copy/paste each teacher’s response into a cell. If you are using a word processing document, copy paste into the document and then use the comments feature. 
2. Read through your data. Read through it again. Know your data before you begin analyzing it.  
3. First cycle coding: 
   - Apply in vivo codes using participants’ exact words when those words seem important, meaning those words that answered the question.
   - Apply descriptive codes to summarize the topic or idea being discussed, again, focusing on the ideas that answer the question.
4. Second cycle coding: 
   - Review your codes for patterns.
   - Group related codes into categories and then themes.
5. Keep notes, or memos, about your thinking throughout the process.
6. Continue with steps 1-5 until you have coded all of your data.

> [!NOTE]
It is best to take a short break between each round of coding to get some distance from your data and help you come back to it with "new" eyes, as it is easy to miss things the longer you focus on your data set.

> [!NOTE]
You may be wondering about using an AI program to analyze your qualitative data. Just like with quantitative data analysis, we warn against using AI programs to do this. Why? As previously mentioned, AI programs are large-language models that use existing data to make predictions based on the existing data in their systems. While AI programs can identify patterns in blocks of text, they lack the ability to think critically and to notice the nuances in human responses. Additionally, there are ethical and privacy concerns when uploading data into an AI program, particularly when copying/pasting human responses into a system where the data is not securely stored. Additionally, AI systems are notorious for both being biased and for reinforcing biased views. Note that some of the above programs do integrate AI into their software; however, the tools, with AI integration, are specifically designed to help you analyze your qualitative data. 

#### Mixed Methods
Mixed methods research uses a combination of quantitative and qualitative data methods. For example, if you distribute an electronic survey to your teachers about collaboration, you might include some questions that have multiple choice answers (closed-ended questions) and some questions that require a longer word response (open-ended response questions). For the closed-ended questions, you would use descriptive statistics (quantitative data analysis) to analyze the data; for example, responses may be analyzed using percentages, averages, or measures of central tendency). For the open-ended questions (where the teachers provide responses using their own words instead of selecting answers from a pre-determined list), you would use qualitative data analysis, coding their responses to identify patterns, categories, and common themes. This type of analysis helps explain participants’ attitudes, experiences, and perspectives in greater depth than the numbers alone can provide. Let’s look at a couple of examples of mixed methods research in your library.  

*Example 1:* You genrefy the fiction section of your library. After one month, you run a circulation statistics report in your library management system. The LMS calculates a 31% increase in fiction circulation over the previous month. This quantitative data indicates that the circulation has increased, but it does not explain why. The increase may have occurred because students found books more easily, or it may simply reflect excitement about the novelty of the change. To better understand the reasons behind the increase, qualitative data should also be collected from students regarding their experiences with the new organization system and how they selected books. 

*Example 2:* When hosting library programs, attendance numbers provide quantitative data about how many individuals attended a program, but they do not reveal whether participants were engaged, enjoyed the experience, learned something new, or would attend future events. A mixed methods survey could include both rating-scale questions and open-ended questions. For example, attendees might rate their engagement on a scale from 1 to 5, allowing the researcher to calculate the mean engagement score (quantitative). They might also identify two strengths of the program and one area for improvement. These written responses would then be coded and analyzed for recurring themes (qualitative).

Another point to consider when analyzing data is external factors (Urban Institute, 2004). If you live in a community where high school football is hugely popular, and you schedule a library event for secondary students during a football game, your attendance numbers may be quite low because you are competing with a popular social event. Also, weather conditions may affect attendance numbers. If it rains, attendance numbers may generally be lower in communities where rain events are rare. You could create and deliver the most amazing program, but if there is a competing event or rainy weather, that may contribute to low turnout. Numbers alone (quantitative) may suggest that your program was not a success, but using qualitative feedback in addition to the usage and circulation statistics could show that the students and parents who did participate really enjoyed it and would attend another event in the future. 


#### Interpreting Findings
Okay, so you have collected data and analyzed it. What’s next? Since data analysis results in findings, now you must interpret the findings, drawing conclusions to determine what the findings tell you, giving them meaning. Findings alone reveal little without understanding what they indicate about the goals or program outcomes. 

Interpreting findings involves drawing conclusions and determining what the results reveal about the effectiveness of a program, intervention, or change. As a librarian engaging in evidence-based practice, you must consider questions such as:
* Did the project meet its intended goals?
* Was the program successful?
* Did the intervention make a meaningful difference?
* What aspects worked well?
* Where do gaps or weaknesses remain?
* What improvements are needed?
* What could be done differently in the future?

An important part of interpretation is providing context for the data. Numbers alone often have little meaning to stakeholders unless they are connected to a larger story. For example, what does “we circulated 22,432 books” actually mean to your principal? Is that an increase from the previous year, a decrease, or about the same? On average, how many books per student does that represent? Again, is that figure improving, declining, or remaining stable over time? Without comparison, explanation, and context, statistics may not clearly communicate the significance of your library’s impact.

Likewise, if you implement an initiative to increase collaboration with teachers, simply reporting “I collaborated with 3 teachers this year” provides little insight into the value of that work. Instead, you should explain the significance of the collaboration. Did those collaborative lessons support student learning outcomes? Did they increase student engagement, improve research skills, or integrate information literacy into classroom instruction? Context helps stakeholders understand not only what was done, but why it matters. School librarians must therefore be intentional and purposeful when presenting evidence and findings. As DiScala (2019) stated, “There’s not much point in collecting evidence just to have it. We collect evidence with a purpose so that we can adjust and improve our practice.”

Referring back to the previous examples in the Mixed Methods subsection, circulation statistics show increased fiction checkouts, which may initially appear successful, but interpretation requires examining the qualitative feedback to determine whether students genuinely benefited from the organizational changes that led to the increased checkouts. Similarly, low attendance at a library program (i.e., findings from your quantitative data analysis) may not necessarily indicate failure if participants reported high levels of engagement and satisfaction (i.e., findings from your qualitative analysis).

Koufogiannakis and Brettle (2016) describe this stage as “adapt,” emphasizing the importance of using findings to inform future decisions and improvements. Interpreting findings allows researchers and practitioners to move beyond simply reporting numbers or themes and instead use evidence to guide meaningful action and decision-making.

> [!NOTE]
Koufogiannakis and Brettle (2016) also caution remaining aware of personal bias during interpretation, as “It is important to remember that we all bring biases to our interpretation of the evidence as a whole, so recognizing this is an important step toward making better decisions” (p. 59). School librarians must ensure they are reporting what the data actually shows rather than what they hope or expect to find. Maintaining objectivity during interpretation helps ensure that conclusions are accurate, credible, and useful for decision-making and future planning.


#### Correlation Is Not Causation
When interpreting research findings, it is important to understand the difference between causation and correlation. Causation means that a specific change or intervention directly caused a particular outcome (A causes B to happen). Correlation means that two factors appear to be related or occur together, but one cannot be confirmed as the direct cause of the other. In library settings, it is often difficult to prove causation because many variables may influence outcomes simultaneously (A and B happen together, but A does not cause B to happen; they are related, but sometimes through a third factor).

For example, imagine a school librarian starts a student book club in September, and by the end of the year, student reading fluency scores increase. Although the increase in scores may be related to participation in the book club, we cannot automatically assume that the book club alone caused the improvement. Other factors may also have contributed to the increase in reading fluency. Reading teachers may have implemented a new instructional curriculum, administrators and teachers may have encouraged parents to support reading at home, and/or the school district may have introduced a healthy breakfast, lunch, and snack program for all students. Because multiple factors can influence student achievement, researchers and practitioners must be cautious about claiming direct causation. Thus, this is an example of correlation. 

However, stronger evidence for causation can be established when you, the librarian, carefully examine specific groups and compare data over time. In the case of the book club, you could compare participating students’ reading fluency scores before and after involvement in the club. You could also compare the literacy scores of students who participated in the book club with those of students who did not participate. If participating students demonstrated greater improvement than nonparticipants, a stronger argument could be made that the book club contributed to improved reading fluency. Additionally, if very few other instructional changes occurred during that same period, the evidence supporting a causal relationship would become even stronger.

Although it may not always be possible to definitively prove causation, it is still important to document efforts toward improvement and communicate results to stakeholders. Librarians should share evidence demonstrating how programs, services, or interventions align with established goals and contribute to student learning or engagement. For example, a librarian might report that students participating in the book club showed increased reading motivation, checked out more books, and demonstrated higher fluency gains compared to nonparticipants. Similarly, a library makerspace program may not be solely responsible for improved problem-solving skills, but student reflections, teacher observations, and increased engagement data may provide evidence that the program positively supported student learning experiences.
