### A Brief History of EBP in School Librarianship
*Suggested time: ## minutes*
<img align="right" src="/img/EBP-History-in-SL1.png" alt="EBP in school librarianship timeline" width="50%"/>
Evidence-based practice in school librarianship has its roots in the medical field (see [Sackett et al. (1996) article](https://www.cebm.ox.ac.uk/files/news-and-views/sackett-evidence-based-medicine.pdf/view) for more information). The goal of evidence-based medicine, as it was called in its beginnings, was to use the best research evidence to guide medical practice and improve the care and, ultimately, health outcomes for patients. The aim for evidence-based practice in education and school librarianship follows in the same vein – to base decisions about our teaching and classroom practices on what we know about our students and the best research and pedagogical practices.

Before EBP was formalized in school librarianship, school librarians relied on standards and their own professional experience to do their jobs. The focus was on building and maintaining a collection that met the varied reading needs and abilities of their student population, promoting a love of reading, and ensuring the school community could access the materials and resources the library offered. It was focused on the library and what it provided rather than the student and what they were learning.

Even though the term “evidence-based practice” was not being used in the school library field, scholars were laying the theoretical and instructional foundations for school librarians’ use of EBP. Patricla Senn Breivik focused on the importance of information literacy as a critical literacy children will need in the 21st century. Carol C. Kuhlthau and Michael B. Eisenberg created and advocated for the use of inquiry models focused on information literacy and the information seeking process. Kuhlthau’s Information Search Process (ISP) and Eisenberg’s Big6 model further solidified the foundation for school librarians to consider their instructional role and impact on student learning.

These theoretical and instructional foundations for information literacy led the way for scholars like Ross Todd (1995) to study the impact of integrating information literacy skills into the school curriculum and the positive learning outcomes of doing so, helping the field to begin thinking about a framework for how school libraries could measure their impact on teaching and learning. Concurrently, scholars focusing on other types of librarianship (public, academic, health, and special) were arguing for the incorporation of evidence-based practice in the field of information science and librarianship (Eldredge, 2000; Booth, 2000; Crumley & Koufogiannakis, 2001). Jonathan Eldredge formally connected the evidence-based practice framework used in the medical field to librarianship, introducing the idea of “evidence-based librarianship” or EBL in his work with health sciences libraries (2000).

The decade between 2000-2010 brought the formalization of EBP into school librarianship as more studies on EBP were conducted and models created. Todd expanded on his previous work to introduce a 3-part evidence framework – evidence for, in, and of practice (2006) – and further defined this model in his 2015 Knowledge Quest article. While Todd and others were advocating for the use of EBP in school librarianship, Keith Curry Lance began leading major statewide studies on the impact school libraries have on student learning and achievement, providing initial empirical research evidence for the work school librarians do everyday. Kulthau’s work on her Information Search Process (ISP) also provided a means for school librarians to understand how students learn through the inquiry process.

The 2010s saw EBP gaining more traction within the field as more scholars began researching the topic (DiScala & Subramaniam, 2011; Lemieux, 2012; [Todd, 2018](https://books.google.com/books?hl=en&lr=&id=yknEEAAAQBAJ&oi=fnd&pg=PA130&dq=%22evidence-based+practice%22+%22school+librarianship%22&ots=yLJ3SQBgcs&sig=wsBBZSC3HykCAtnhPR-JlV1mCws#v=onepage&q=%22evidence-based%20practice%22%20%22school%20librarianship%22&f=false)). During this time, scholars researched how EBP was being conducted in school libraries, how it was being taught to new school librarians, the various challenges to incorporating it, and school librarians’ understandings of EBP (Cahill & Richey, 2012; [Richey & Cahill, 2014](https://www.ala.org/sites/default/files/aasl/content/aaslpubsandjournals/slr/vol17/SLR_EvidenceBasedLibrary_V17.pdf)). Researchers saw the value and practical application of EBP as an answer to the field’s struggle to remain relevant amidst the growing use of the Internet as people’s primary information source. There was also a shift in the focus and purpose of school libraries from the library space and collection/resources to what the students learned in and through the library; the field was beginning to see that their role in the school community was less about the “stuff” housed in the library and more about how the library meets students’ various information literacy and research learning needs. As a result, researchers and practitioners began sharing different ways they gathered student data and evidence of their learning to improve their own practice and advocate for the library program.

This current decade has seen EBP in school librarianship continue its examination of EBP practices in the field ([Moore & Cahill, 2022](https://repository.ifla.org/rest/api/core/bitstreams/ae44dce9-eb57-4652-9c55-847bbe03f0f2/content)) in addition to expanding its focus to consider how the library meets the needs of all learners and helps students navigate and learn in digital environments where a more nuanced and keen eye for information credibility is needed.

### What is EBP?
> “National evidence from school library research is tied to and exemplified by local evidence. The challenge for the profession is to move beyond simply reporting state studies to crafting a compelling narrative that starts with local evidence of practice and links to the wider formal research evidence for practice. Local evidence narrative is the missing piece.” (Todd, 2015)

**READ** the [2015 Ross Todd article](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1048950.pdf) that defines the 3-part framework for evidence-based practice in school librarianship

#### A 4-Stage Process
In this curriculum, we present a method for implementing what Todd calls “evidence of practice.” Evidence of practice is primarily focused on student outcomes and evidence of learning; it’s about what students know as a result of your teaching and the activities you lead in the library. We will walk through a 4-stage process to gather, analyze, and communicate evidence of student learning in the library. Although EBP is not linear, we present EBP in this way to help cover and consider all the aspects of EBP.
1. **OBSERVE/REFLECT/ASK:** The first stage in applying EBP is to ask good questions about how what you do in and through the school library connects to what students are doing in their classrooms. (EXAMPLE: How does my 6th grade unit on the inquiry method help students with their research report on Ancient Egypt?)
1. **GATHER/ACQUIRE:** In the second stage, you will collect the data that will best answer the question(s) you identified in Stage 1.
1. **ANALYZE & INTERPRET:** In Stage 3, you will look deeper at the data you collected and analyze it for patterns, ETC. Based on your analysis of your data, you will begin interpreting the data and begin formulating answers to the questions you asked in Stage 1.
1. <p style="color: blue"> **COMMUNICATE & REFLECT** </p> on process: In the 4th stage, you will translate your evidence into a format that will engage and capture the attention of your audience, namely, those in your school community like your principal, teachers, students, and their parents. You will also reflect on how/if anything in your practice needs to change based on your findings and evidence (e.g. if you found that a certain sub-population of students still struggled to find information on Ancient Egypt after you taught them how to access and use various databases, what might you change in future iterations of this lesson?)

### Importance of EBP
Integrating EBP into your work as a school librarian serves two purposes: (1) helping you improve the school library services and resources to meet the varied needs of the community of learners it serves and (2) showcasing the value of the library program and the difference it makes to the teaching and learning in the school. As a school librarian, your job is to ensure that the library resources and activities support and meet the curricular goals of the school/district. These two purposes really go hand-in-hand. If you are using evidence and data of student learning to guide how the library functions and provides for the informational needs of the school community, you can communicate this to your principal, the teachers, parents, and students. The connection between what the library does and the school’s overall goals will be clearer and you can make the case for the library’s existence and needs better.

### Importance of Planning for EBP
Benjamin Franklin is attributed with the popular proverb, “If you fail to plan, you are planning to fail.” This statement also applies to implementing EBP. Integrating EBP into your practice will not automatically happen because you want it to. It takes concerted effort, planning, and thought to not only begin to include EBP into your work, but to continue with it long-term. Not only will planning help sustain your EBP into the future, but effective planning will also make the entire process less daunting and scary.

### Begin with Your Why
Our own personal and professional values and motivations guide our actions in everything we do as a school librarian. Everything you do as a school librarian, including your implementation of EBP, communicates your values and beliefs about your role as a school librarian. Are your stated values and beliefs enacted through what you do daily in the library? Or is there a disconnect between what you believe and what you do each day? More importantly, can others identify what you value through observing what you do in the library every day? Therefore, it is imperative for your practice in the library to accurately reflect what you believe about your role as a school librarian.

Oftentimes, we tend to focus on the things we do and the services we provide to the school (e.g. teach information literacy classes, curate and manage the library collection/resources, and “other duties as assigned”) rather than why we do those things. If you want to implement EBP into your practice as a school librarian, a good place to start is by identifying your overall purpose for your work as a school librarian. When you have a clear purpose for the work you do, you are better able to identify how the library program fits into the overall school ecosystem and gauge its effectiveness.
