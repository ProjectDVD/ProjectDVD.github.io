### Module 2: Collecting Evidence of Practice
#### Introduction: What is evidence?
*Suggested time: ## minutes*

It goes without saying that evidence is fundamental in evidence-based practice. But what is evidence? Is it the same as data? More importantly, how do you collect it so you can use it to evaluate, improve, and promote the library program in your school?

The line between ‘evidence’ and ‘data’ is a bit blurry. In your conversations with other educators, you may have used these two words interchangeably. [DeWitt & Nelson (2024)](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/opinion-the-difference-between-data-and-evidence-what-school-leaders-need-to-know/2024/12) identify the distinction between evidence and data:
> “Data refer to raw facts, figures, or information collected from observations, measurements, surveys, tests, or other sources. In an educational setting, data can include test scores, attendance records, survey responses, or classroom-observation notes. Evidence refers to data that have been analyzed, interpreted, or used to support a claim, conclusion, or decision. Evidence goes beyond the raw numbers to show meaningful patterns, trends, or findings that can inform action.”

Based on these definitions for evidence and data, what we are really collecting is data. Data then becomes evidence once you analyze and interpret it to share with others.

#### Show Me the Data
There are essentially two types of data you can collect to use as evidence of your practice: quantitative and qualitative data.

|            |  Quantitative Data  |  Qualitative Data  |
| ---------- |  -----------------  |  ----------------  |
| Definition | - "Deductive in nature, generally uses numerical data, and are seen as more structured and objective” (Connaway & Radford, 2021)
- Involves counting and measuring something (can do math with this data)  |  Non-numerical information
| Methods to collect | - Survey/questionnaire (multiple choice, rating scale questions)
- Running collection reports through OPAC*
- Usage statistics: Student book/material check-out reports*
- Number of classes taught*
- Student quizzes/tests/assessments
- Student polls | - Survey/questionnaire (open-ended, short answer questions)
- Interviews/focus groups
- Observations
- Student-generated reflections
- Photographs/videos

*NOTE: These are data that librarians have historically collected that, on their own, have little to no connection to student learning. This doesn't mean you shouldn't collect these types of data, but you will want to ensure that the data you collect has a purpose and connects to the larger story you want to tell about the library program.

#### Collecting Data Twice
In the EBP process, data collection occurs at two points: prior to planning and after implementing. During the planning process, you may collect evidence such as student demographics, standardized or benchmark test scores, professional standards, and similar projects completed by other librarians and/or teachers. These types of evidence may serve as a guide to inform your plan. After you implement your change, you will collect other types of evidence to measure the efficacy of your program or change. The type of data collected depends on your goal(s). Examples can be found in the “Show Me the Data” section.

If you collect both qualitative and quantitative data, the research world calls this “mixed methods.” Sometimes when you implement a data collection method you can collect both qualitative and quantitative data at the same time (example: a survey with both multiple choice (quantitative) and open-ended (qualitative) questions). Sometimes you collect quantitative data that later leads to collecting related qualitative data (example: ADD), and sometimes you collect qualitative data that later leads to collecting related quantitative data (example: ADD).

> **AI in Data Collection**
> For the most part, the data you collect will come directly from your students and/or teachers. However, you may consider using AI in data collection if you want to:
> - look up annual testing data for your school and/or district
> - generate ideas on what data you could collect based on your unique situation
> - create a draft of the tool (survey, pre-/post-test, interview question(s), etc.) you will use to collect data
> BENEFITS of using AI in data collection:
> - ENHANCING EFFICIENCY: AI can save you a lot of time in preparing for data collection
> - YOUR "CO-RESEARCHER": AI can serve as a brainstorming “partner” when you want to generate ideas on what data you could collect

> CAUTIONS of using AI in data collection:
> - SECURITY/PRIVACY: Because AI pulls information from the web, you will not be able to receive specific test scores for individual students at your school, as that is protected confidential information. Similarly, you should not input any personal student data into AI as that violates FERPA and CIPA laws.
> - ERROR PRONE: AI is not infallible. It can (and often does) make mistakes. You **must** double-check all AI outputs, which could negate any time saved by using AI in the first place.
