### Module 4: Effectively Communicating Evidence of Practice
*Suggested time: ## minutes*

To effectively communicate evidence of your practice, it is useful to understand and think about the following:
1. How your stakeholders communicate so you can communicate in ways that “speak to” and resonate with them
2. Your message and what it is you actually want to say about the library program
3. Which communication technique would best serve in telling a story with your data/evidence

#### YOUR AUDIENCE: WITH WHOM ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?

Communication has been a formal academic field for about 100 years, with roots in rhetoric led by thinkers such as Aristotle and Plato. From all the research done in this field, there is a general understanding that humans do not all communicate in the same way. Many factors contribute to how we communicate with others: our cultural background, family history, and individual personalities, to name a few. When it comes to communicating in a work environment, Williams and Miller (2013) posit that “people frequently use a one-size-fits-all approach when trying to convince their bosses, peers, and staff” (p. 6) rather than tailoring their communication and using language that speaks powerfully to the unique people they are trying to persuade. [In a 2022 interview on the Think Fast, Talk Smart podcast](https://www.fastersmarter.io/54-leadership-and-ethics-how-to-communicate-your-core-values/), Ken Shotts, a political science professor at Stanford University, noted that the best communication advice he has learned is to always remember that “your audience is not yourself.” Their motives, values, opinions, likes, and dislikes are different from your own. Therefore, if you want to influence others’ behavior, you need to understand what they care about and what motivates them.

Much of the advice on communicating and presenting to others has one common thread: **know your audience**.

#### YOUR MESSAGE: WHAT ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?

We hope the data analysis and interpretation you did in Module 3 provided evidence of how the school library program positively impacts the learning and teaching happening in your school. Before jumping headfirst into communicating everything that you have learned from analyzing your data with your audience, it’s important for you to know and be able to integrate your data/evidence into the overall message you want to communicate to your audience. If you want to influence and impact your audience, simply sharing your evidence is not enough. It must be connected to the overall message you want to communicate, otherwise you are simply sharing facts that have no real meaning or purpose.

What is the message you want to communicate with your audience? The message you want to share ideally includes the *<u>value</u>* of the school library program, rather than simply information or the *<u>features</u>* about the library or a specific library program you want to promote. “What is the **ONE CORE IDEA** you want others to know about the school library?” and “What is the **LASTING IMPRESSION** you want people to have about the library program after visiting it or speaking with you about it?” are questions to ask yourself to get to the heart of the library’s value and importance to the school community. It is only after you are able to articulate the core idea or message that you can begin to incorporate the data you’ve collected and analyzed into the overall message you want to share about the school library program.

> [!NOTE] Use the [Minto Pyramid](https://untools.co/minto-pyramid/) as a way to formulate your message to your desired audience.

**<u>READ</u>** [this article from the Thunderbird School of Global Management at Arizona State University](https://thunderbird.asu.edu/thought-leadership/insights/value-creation-how-communicate-value). Think about the school library program as the “product or service” and the teachers, students, parents, administrators as “the customers.”

**<u>REFLECT</u>**
Spend some time considering the answers to the following questions:
* What is **<u>ONE BENEFIT</u>** of the school library that nothing else in the school provides?
* What is the **<u>MAIN THING</u>** you want others to know about the school library program? Be as specific as possible and focus on what’s in it for them (i.e. fulfilling a need).
* Now, connect the main benefit of the library to what your chosen audience cares about the most. What is **<u>ONE THING</u>** your audience cares deeply about?

#### YOUR METHOD: HOW ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?

> “When sharing evidence, librarians should consider the best means of communicating in ways that enable others to receive their messages, not just the words but rather the intent and significance of the message being shared.” (Moore et al., 2023, 9)

Once you know your audience and the message you want to communicate, you now need to consider how (and through what medium) you will communicate that message. Our digitally connected landscape affords us with countless ways to communicate messages to our intended audience(s). From email to text messaging, video conferencing to in-person meetings, each communication format has benefits and limitations. Social media sites allow you to create content for a potentially huge audience that includes your intended audience, but you are (mostly) confined by the parameters of the platform. In-person meetings foster opportunities for immediate feedback and brainstorming through back-and-forth conversations, but scheduling these meetings can be difficult if they aren’t already pre-scheduled on everyone’s calendars.

Here are some media that you can use in your communications with your audience(s):

Similar to crafting your message, considering your audience is crucial when choosing a platform in which to share your message. If you want to get the attention of your principal, for instance, but she does not have time in her schedule to meet with you, you will need to be strategic and creative in how you go about communicating with her. To get the attention of a specific audience, consider where their attention is already spent and communicate through that method (e.g. if your principal is on social media, posting about an activity you are doing in the library to the school library’s social media account is likely to get their attention).

Not all communication methods are created equal. Being intentional and strategic in how and when you communicate with your stakeholder(s) is key. Information overload is real. For instance, if your go-to format for sharing all the amazing things happening in the library is email, unless you get a reply, use a read receipt setting, or install a browser extension, it is hard to know if your emails are read by the intended recipients. Chances are, the people you email have overflowing email inboxes and yours may only be skimmed or, worse, unread, despite all the time and effort you put into it (Moore et al., 2023). If you know this is the case with those you want to communicate with, email, while it may be the easiest option, may not be the best way to share your evidence of practice.

> **AI in Communicating Evidence**
> 
> Using AI to help you in communicating your evidence of practice has both positives and negatives. AI could be useful in creating talking points for a meeting you’re having with a specific stakeholder/group to share about the school library’s impact. But on the other hand, it lacks the voice, personality, and passion that you would bring to your presentation. Additionally, you want to make sure you don’t rely solely on AI to do all the work for you as this could lessen your credibility as a professional with your audience and others might begin to question what else is AI generated. We are speaking to the choir, but also remember that any AI that you do use in your presentation materials **must be cited**.
