### Module 4: Effectively Communicating Evidence of Practice

To effectively communicate evidence of your practice, it is useful to understand and think about the following:
1. Different ways that people communicate, generally
2. How your stakeholders communicate specifically
   * Communicating in ways that “speak to” and resonate with your audience/stakeholder group
   * Getting your audience’s attention (getting a “seat at the table”)
3. Your message – Knowing what it is you want to communicate
4. Various communication techniques
   * Tell a story with your data/evidence
   * Use “social math”

#### YOUR AUDIENCE: WITH WHOM ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?
*Suggested time: ## minutes*

Communication has been a formal academic field for about 100 years, with roots in rhetoric led by thinkers such as Aristotle and Plato. From all the research done in this field, there is a general understanding that humans do not all communicate in the same way. Many factors contribute to how we communicate with others: our cultural background, family history, and individual personalities, to name a few. When it comes to communicating in a work environment, Williams and Miller (2013) posit that “people frequently use a one-size-fits-all approach when trying to convince their bosses, peers, and staff” rather than tailoring their communication and using language that speaks powerfully to the unique people they are trying to persuade. [In a 2022 interview on the Think Fast, Talk Smart podcast](https://www.fastersmarter.io/54-leadership-and-ethics-how-to-communicate-your-core-values/), Ken Shotts, a political science professor at Stanford University, noted that the best communication advice he has learned is to always remember that “your audience is not yourself.” Their motives, values, opinions, likes, and dislikes are different from your own. Therefore, if you want to influence others’ behavior, you need to understand what they care about and what motivates them.

Much of the advice on communicating and presenting to others has one common thread: **know your audience**.

#### YOUR MESSAGE: WHAT ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?

We hope the data analysis you did in **Module ##** provided evidence of how the school library program positively impacts the learning and teaching happening in your school. Before jumping headfirst into communicating everything that you have learned from analyzing your data with your audience, it’s important for you to know and be able to integrate your data/evidence into the overall message you want to communicate to your audience. If you want to influence and impact your audience, simply sharing your evidence is not enough. It must be connected to the overall message you want to communicate, otherwise you are simply sharing facts that have no real meaning or purpose.

What is the message you want to communicate with your audience? The message you want to share ideally includes the *<u>value</u>* of the school library program, rather than simply information or the *<u>features</u>* about the library or a specific library program you want to promote. “What is the **ONE CORE IDEA** you want others to know about the school library?” and “What is the **LASTING IMPRESSION** you want people to have about the library program after visiting it or speaking with you about it?” are questions to ask yourself to get to the heart of the library’s value and importance to the school community. It is only after you are able to articulate the core idea or message that you can begin to incorporate the data you’ve collected and analyzed into the overall message you want to share about the school library program.

#### YOUR METHOD: HOW ARE YOU COMMUNICATING?

Once you know your audience and the message you want to communicate, you now need to consider how (and through what medium) you will communicate that message. Our digitally connected landscape affords us with countless ways to communicate messages to our intended audience(s). From email to text messaging, video conferencing to in-person meetings, each communication medium has benefits and limitations. Social media sites allow you to create content for a potentially large audience that includes your intended audience, but you are (mostly) confined by the parameters of the platform. In-person meetings foster opportunities for immediate feedback and brainstorming through back-and-forth conversations, but scheduling these meetings can be difficult if they aren’t already pre-scheduled on everyone’s calendars. 

Similar to crafting your message, considering your audience is crucial when choosing a platform in which to share your message. If you want to get the attention of your principal, for instance, but she does not have time in her schedule to meet with you, you will need to be strategic and creative in how you go about communicating with her. To get the attention of a specific audience, consider where their attention is already spent and communicate through that method (e.g. if your principal is on social media, post about an activity you are doing in the library to the school library’s social media account).
